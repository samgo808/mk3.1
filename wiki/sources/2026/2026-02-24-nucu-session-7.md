# NUCU Session 7: Aging, Healthcare and Dignity in Japan — 2026-02-24

The archived February 24, 2026 BAIM 3300 session, “Japan's Aging Society & Healthcare in Japan,” features **Hasegawa-sensei** and a contribution from **Sawako Tanaka** of Nagoya University's entrepreneurship program. It asks how healthcare, long-term care and community support help people live well, especially near life's end. This is a record of a lecture and documentary excerpts, not current medical, insurance or legal guidance.

## Documentary and Dr. Ryoichi Ichihashi

The NHK/Japan Broadcasting Corporation material asks what constitutes a good death. It cites **over 1.5 million annual deaths** and a survey in which **nearly 30%** do not view longevity as a good thing. Later classroom paraphrases describe the figure as older people lacking hope; that is not the same survey claim, and neither the instrument nor population is supplied. The program refers to **100 years of Japanese broadcasting**; the lecturer's broadcast-year wording (“2020 for last year”) is unclear.

**Dr. Ryoichi Ichihashi**, also presented in Japanese order as **Ichihashi Ryoichi**, is described as a **Nagoya University School of Medicine graduate** who moved from **hematology to home medical care**. His team is said to include around **100 professionals**: doctors, nurses, dentists, dieticians and a music therapist. **More than 1,000 visitors** reportedly came to observe or train. Another excerpt says he cares for **about 50 patients**; the transcript does not say whether that is his personal caseload or the clinic total.

A swallowing/meal example shows dentists and dieticians assessing chewing and swallowing and adjusting food texture and size to the person's wishes. Another vignette illustrates asking a person what small activity would make life meaningful and coordinating an outing. **[Redacted: patient identity and identifying medical/life-history details from the documentary excerpts.]** The raw evidence remains local. These examples teach person-centered care and hope, not treatment instructions or authority to disclose a patient's story.

The documentary describes Ichihashi as **strongly opposed to euthanasia**, then the transcript calls him a doctor “specializing in euthanasia.” This is a direct conflict and likely transcription error; it must not become his professional description. A visit to the **Netherlands** is connected with encouraging patients to assess their condition and express wishes, not with a claim that he practices euthanasia. The account distinguishes quality of life from extending life at any cost.

## Medical-system history and organization

The lecture dates adoption of Western medicine to the **late 1700s**, modernization to the **Meiji Restoration** (garbled in transcription), and **universal health coverage to 1961**. It links access, predictable patient costs and community networks with strong health outcomes. Longer life also increases chronic-care and family-support needs.

The source says long-term residents join public insurance regardless of age, income or medical history. Employees and employers share premiums; patients generally pay part of the bill. Easy access to clinics and hospitals is contrasted with stricter gatekeeping elsewhere. Extra services such as private rooms may be outside standard cover. These are broad lecture descriptions, not a complete statement of eligibility, referral exceptions or visitor insurance.

The lecture gives the following cost-sharing examples:

| Source description | Lecture figure and limitation |
|---|---|
| Children under six | Usually **20%**, potentially **0%** after local subsidy. |
| Nagoya children's care | Local government described as covering treatment through around **18/high-school age**; exact age wording is imprecise. |
| Most adults | Usually **30% patient / 70% insurance**. |
| Older adults | “**70 to 84**” at **20%**, then **75 and over** often **10%**; bands overlap and conflict. |
| High-income seniors | May pay more; no complete schedule supplied. |
| Separate 75+ system | Introduced in **2008**, with income-based premiums; later “**3%**” patient share conflicts with the earlier **10%**. |

Japan is described as using a nationwide **fee schedule**, making base prices more controlled than fragmented US hospital/insurer/network pricing. A surgery, **seven-day admission** and tests are examples, but exact comparative prices are absent. “30% of what?” emphasizes that the base price matters as much as the copay rate.

The source describes an **income-related monthly cap** in Japan. A hypothetical US plan has a **$2,000 deductible**, **20% coinsurance** and **$8,000 out-of-pocket maximum**, with network rules affecting payment. This is an illustration, not a typical or guaranteed policy. Affordable visits do not mean every older person can afford repeated care, facilities and living costs; pension discussion is partly unintelligible and supplies no dependable benefit amount.

## Long-Term Care Insurance and care settings

The speaker initially says **2008**, then explicitly corrects **Long-Term Care Insurance** to **2000**. That correction is preserved; the separate 75+ medical scheme is still dated 2008. LTC coverage is described for people **65+** and some younger people with specified age-related conditions; the younger range is garbled as “age 64,” so a lower limit is not invented. Taxes and premiums share costs across society. Assessment assigns a **care level** (transcribed “care rental”), and users are said to pay **10–30%**.

Needs include bathing, dressing, eating and safe movement. Home-based services include nursing, home help, meals and day/community programs. Three reasons favor home care: personal preference and familiarity, potentially lower cost than full-time facilities, and insufficient nursing-home capacity with waiting lists. Home care is not always possible: **24-hour/seven-day support** or an overwhelmed family may require a facility.

Ichihashi is described as bringing equipment, including **mobile X-ray**, to the home. Other equipment names are unintelligible and not guessed. Public special nursing homes, transcribed “Tokyo,” are described as less costly but with longer waits; private facilities may have more openings and amenities but cost more. The Japanese facility term is unresolved. Home-death preference versus hospital-death figures appear as “more than **8 percent**” in both directions; no reliable percentages can be recovered.

## Sustainability and opportunities

The pressures are a population with nearly **30% over 65**, fewer workers paying taxes and premiums, staff overwork, repeated visits for minor needs, and uneven distribution of clinics. A cold patch for knee/leg pain illustrates potential overuse. The lecturer argues that freedom to open clinics and seek treatment improves convenience while encouraging urban concentration and spending. Comparisons with **UK and German** approval systems are unverified legal generalizations.

The innovation brief is not to replace clinicians, but reduce friction and workload while keeping care safe and human. Named opportunity areas are **hospital-to-home coordination, caregiver support, workforce efficiency and mobility**. Patients, families and teams may experience different pain points. The session asks where the biggest need is, who feels it, and what could help in real settings.

## Q&A: adoption, transport and medical staffing

Technology adoption can be slow because clinics lack budget or people to implement it, and older patients may struggle with the interface. Subsidies alone do not make a tool usable. The response to the fax-versus-advanced-technology question emphasizes cost and fit rather than assuming novelty will be adopted.

Cities are described as having buses, subways, trains, taxis and easier access to community halls or enjoyable activities. Rural areas have fewer services; older drivers may resist surrendering licenses because a car supports independence. The speaker describes encouraging voluntary surrender, cheaper taxis, small community buses, younger residents giving rides and autonomous-bus trials. The transcript does not identify trial locations or establish licensing requirements. Government-supported community events are another avenue for connection.

A Q&A question cites **about 2.5 doctors per 1,000 people**, with comparisons to the US and China. Earlier the lecturer describes fewer doctors than many OECD countries, then later roughly similar ratios. These comparisons are not resolved. The answer stresses workforce distribution and specialty choice: young doctors are said to prefer other fields over geriatrics; government or medical-society scholarships/incentives are discussed. No exact award scheme is named.

A **March** international meeting with a geriatrician from the **University of North Carolina** and Nagoya is announced as planned. Rural responses include universities sending doctors and public incentives for healthcare and other workers. Neither a future meeting nor a proposed intervention is recorded here as completed.

She calls the previous year's CU collaboration the first and welcomes the 2026 return. The unclear regional name is not silently resolved.
