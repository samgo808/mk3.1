# NUCU Session 2: Edo to Meiji Reinvention — 2026-01-20

The archived January 20, 2026 BAIM 3300 lecture, “Reinvention Under Pressure: Institutional Transformation Japan from Edo to Meiji (Pre-war): A Case Study,” presents Japan's reinvention through a business-school lens. Samuel Goodman compares isolation, imported technology and institutional reform, preparing students for later postwar and contemporary-Japan lectures. This page records lecture claims and classroom arguments, not an independently verified history.

## Edo institutions and first contact

The lecture dates Edo to **1603–1868** and compares the figurehead emperor, shogun, daimyo, samurai and peasants to a hierarchy: shogun as CEO, regional warlords as managers, samurai as warrior/middle-management class, farmers as labor force. These are teaching analogies, not literal corporate roles.

A **1543** Portuguese trading ship, described as storm-driven onto a southern island, introduces two matchlock guns. The daimyo reportedly pays **80 pounds of silver**, estimated in class at **$100,000 total / $50,000 each**, and swordsmiths reverse-engineer the weapons within **one year**. The demonstration shows loading gunpowder, a burning match and unreliable ignition; it illustrates transfer and adaptation, not safe firearm instructions. A passing claim dates gunpowder to the **seventh century**; it is unverified and should not become a settled historical fact.

Goodman links travel to exchanging goods and then ideas, using Colorado House International's **“travel, trade, learn”** slogan. **Francis Xavier** arrives in **1549**, stays **two years**, and the lecture estimates **300,000 Japanese Christians by 1600**. Christianity's equality before God is framed as threatening feudal authority despite profitable trade. The lecture dates missionary expulsion/crackdown to **1614**.

## Rebellion, exclusion and sakoku

A **1637** famine and taxes paid in rice help motivate rebellion. The account names **Amakusa Shiro**, described as **18**, **37,000** insurgents including masterless samurai (**ronin**), an abandoned castle, a **four-month siege**, and **125,000** government troops assisted by Dutch ship cannon. It reports a final assault on **April 12**, execution of all defenders, and Shiro's head displayed at Nagasaki; it does not supply a clear year for that day. Ages, counts and chronology remain lecture claims.

The lecture describes closed borders for roughly **200 years**, dated **1639–1853**, while explicitly acknowledging Dutch and Chinese contact through **Dejima in Nagasaki**. **Fumi-e** required stepping on Christian images to test loyalty. The period is compared with the US founding (**1776**), French Revolution (**1789**) and Industrial Revolution abroad. The claims of “complete isolation,” peace/stagnation and **97% ethnically Japanese** today are sweeping lecture generalizations; the admitted trading exceptions already qualify complete isolation, and no demographic dataset is supplied.

In **1853**, **four black US warships** under **Perry** enter Edo Bay; the lecture says he promises to return in a year with **eight ships**. Steam and cannon superiority underpin **gunboat diplomacy**, followed by the **Convention of Kanagawa**, unequal bargaining and the end of sakoku. The lecture links this to US Manifest Destiny and a crisis of shogunal legitimacy.

## Innovation during isolation

The lecture also describes domestic creativity: woodblock printing (**ukiyo-e**, transcribed “yukio-e”), the **Wave of Kanagawa**, **nigiri sushi as fast food**, early **manga/visual storytelling**, and the **Myriad Clock** showing Western and seasonal Japanese time. The source says seasonal time meant not every day was exactly **24 hours**; this wording confuses clock-hour conventions with day length and needs qualification. It also glosses **e** in ukiyo-e as painting. Mechanical tea-serving and archer dolls illustrate artistic automation. The explanation contrasts peaceful artistic mechanisms with industrial steam engines abroad and speculates about connections to Japanese views of robots as possessing spirit. This is a cultural interpretation, not proof about all Japanese people or robot consciousness.

## Meiji institutional transformation

Meiji is dated **1868–1912**. Southern domains ally with the emperor, push the shogun's forces toward **Hokkaido**, and centralize the archipelago; the lecture highlights **1871**. Daimyo are described as compensated with **government bonds**, and samurai stipends as consuming **50% of the state budget**. Sword restrictions, stipend changes, and a conscript army trained with firearms replace hereditary warrior privilege.

A national **yen** and currency-based taxes, highlighted at **1873**, replace the fragmented rice-tax system. The explanation for financing modernization is centralized collection from farmers and merchants plus industrial development. The **Iwakura Mission** (transcribed “Iokura”) studies US/European banks, laws, factories and constitutions. High-paid foreign experts (**oyatoi gaikokujin**, phonetically garbled in the transcript) train the workforce, then leave as local expertise grows. The slogan is **“Japanese spirit, Western technology.”**

Railways, telegraphs, banking modeled on the **US and Belgium**, a constitution/parliament, and **four-year primary education including girls** accompany reform. The lecture associates schooling with literacy, communication and a workforce for factories, and says it was modeled on **Prussia**. Its description of Prussia as “Germany and Russia” is a source error/oversimplification requiring review, not a definition to teach unqualified.

Government shipbuilding and textile enterprises are privatized to trusted families, introducing **zaibatsu** (transcribed “Zybotts”). **Mitsubishi, Mitsui** and **Nintendo**, founded **1889** as a playing-card company, are examples; the source's company-origin generalizations are not verified corporate histories. The **1877 Satsuma Rebellion** is framed as resistance to samurai displacement and perceived loss of identity, defeated by the new army. Its guns-versus-swords account is simplified.

The lecture connects modernization to empire building, mentioning war with China in **1894**, Korea/Manchuria, Russia in **1905**, victories over larger armies, and a later League of Nations seat. Territorial chronology and the claim that Japan was the only Asian League member require historical verification. World Wars I and II are intentionally not covered in depth. The summary calls the change roughly **40 years** from feudal society to industrial global power.

## Questions, examples and disagreements

- Was sakoku effective sovereignty/risk management or a route to technological obsolescence? Students note that success depends on whether the goal is cultural continuity or development.
- What businesses resist outside disruption? Examples include tariffs on Chinese EVs, a student's Colombia trade account, and the instructor's interpretation of **Google**, search revenue and the transformer as an **innovator's dilemma**. These are discussion claims, not verified current trade policy or corporate motives.
- Is stripping samurai privileges justified to fund national development? Discussion weighs compensation, transition pathways, social costs and foreign-domination risks; the **Opium Wars** and unequal British/Chinese treaties illustrate the latter.
- Is samurai displacement comparable with AI job displacement? Students explicitly challenge the analogy: a state abolishing a hereditary caste differs from diffuse technology affecting many occupations. Others discuss identity, creative writing and automation, and suggest industrialization as a better comparator. A student's “10% or less” samurai-population estimate is unverified.
- Observe shrines beside skyscrapers, consumer behavior, etiquette and attitudes toward robots during the trip; these are reflection prompts, not graded answers.

Further resources named are **Shogun by James Clavell** and its **FX adaptation**, **The Making of Modern Japan**, **The Autobiography of Yukichi Fukuzawa**, and talks by **Sarah Paine** (transcribed “Payne,” institution attribution unverified). Fukuzawa's image on older **10,000-yen notes**, nicknamed “one/two Yukichi,” is a memorable currency example. The lecture's “only Western samurai” claim about the Shogun inspiration is unverified. Goodman says he has not finished Shogun or read the recommended autobiography. The isolated **Bushido/chivalry codification** remark likewise remains a lecture claim.
