# Japan Postwar Growth and Stagnation in NUCU — 2026

The January 27, 2026 NUCU lecture presents Japan's postwar growth and lost decades as a case in institutional adaptation. It connects coordinated investment, technology acquisition and employment loyalty with rapid growth, then examines debt, rigidity and local optimization. The causal account and statistics are the lecturer's interpretation, not current financial guidance or independently verified economic evidence.

## Growth system

**MITI**, the Ministry of International Trade and Industry, selects industries such as steel, shipbuilding, electronics and autos. **FILP**, the Fiscal Investment and Loan Program, channels savings toward corporate investment. Licensing foreign technology while restricting foreign competitors supports domestic learning and exports in this account.

Visionary founders complement public policy. **Akio Morita/Sony** illustrates long-term brand building through rejection of a **100,000-unit Bulova** relabeling deal; inconsistent product details remain in the source's cautions. The **Walkman** illustrates technology that changes behavior, not merely better components. The **salaryman** compact exchanges loyalty and intensive work for expected lifetime employment; it is not universal coverage or a contractual promise to present-day students.

The lecture describes roughly **10% annual growth**, then a transition from postwar devastation to the world's second-largest economy. Its numerical comparisons require checking before use as economic facts; the source page preserves all figures with their limitations.

## Bubble and trapped resources

Land appreciation supports loans; loans buy more assets; rising asset values support more loans. The policy response raises rates and restricts lending. The lecturer argues tightening was too abrupt, and reports widespread wealth loss, layoffs and bankruptcies.

Banks continue funding **zombie companies** to avoid realizing losses. This helps explain capital trapped in weak firms, reduced risk-taking and a broken employment bargain. The source's **1991–2001** lost-decade label is a reference period; the speaker notes arguments that stagnation lasted longer.

## Local excellence can inhibit global adaptation

**Galapagos syndrome** describes sophisticated products optimized for a domestic environment. **i-mode** email, QR, payments and TV precede comparable smartphone ubiquity, but local infrastructure limits exportability. The analogy is about ecosystem fit rather than lack of technical skill.

**Monozukuri** craftsmanship and consensus can encourage quality while delaying release. Sony/Blu-ray versus Netflix/streaming and Japanese engine refinement versus Tesla/software illustrate changes in what creates value. The lecture contrasts Japan's rounded **125-million** market with South Korea's **51-million** market and Samsung/LG's export orientation. These are source-time figures and interpretations.

## Discussion rather than a single policy answer

Students weigh social stability against global risk-taking, lifetime employment against job mobility, and job security against AI-driven displacement. The comparison with Edo is qualified: organizational barriers differ from state-enforced isolation. April recruiting cohorts, fax machines, IC transit cards and niche vending machines become prompts to observe what a society chooses to optimize.
