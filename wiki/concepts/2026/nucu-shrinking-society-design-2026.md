# NUCU Designing for a Shrinking Society — 2026

The archived 2026 NUCU lectures ask students to design technology that helps people adapt to demographic change. The February 17 lecture and April 7 sprint debrief explicitly distinguish this from trying to solve population collapse itself. A smaller population can still need better care, mobility, work-life balance and social connection.

## Understand the linked pressures

Session 6 links weak fertility with marriage patterns, household costs, unequal care burdens, long working hours and weakened lifetime-employment institutions. Rural youth migration compounds aging by removing workers and customers from local schools, transport and care services. Cheap empty houses do not create jobs or social networks by themselves.

The lecture's **2.1 replacement threshold**, approximately **1.2 Japan / 1.6 US / 0.7 South Korea** fertility comparison, and inverted-pyramid illustration are teaching claims with missing reference years. Birth/death counts and the per-minute decline claim do not reconcile cleanly. Use [[2026-02-17-nucu-session-6]] for the full numerical caveats, not as current demographic measurement.

## Start with the person and the task

The market examples range from **Unicharm adult diapers** and **Ito En thicker tea** to solo dining and **Houseboat Club** funeral services. They show markets responding to changed needs; they do not establish that every response improves wellbeing. Ohitorisama describes solo lifestyles, while karoshi describes death by overwork. Solitary death, rural isolation and shrinking family support are social problems as well as business opportunities.

Session 7 asks **where the pain point is, who feels it most and what would actually help**. Care coordination, caregiver support, staff workload and transport can matter more than a novel robot. Home care can honor a person's wishes while increasing the family's burden. An affordable device still fails if older users or a short-staffed clinic cannot use it.

## Preserve human connection

Session 6's **kintsugi** metaphor treats repair as part of an object's history: lacquer with gold or silver makes the join visible. “Shrink gracefully” similarly asks whether society can adapt without abandoning dignity or cohesion. Students questioned robot-only elder care because a caregiver may be someone's main social contact, while seeing value in help with dishes or transport.

Discussion did not settle whether demographic decline is failure or a sustainable adjustment. One argument faults work structures that prevent family formation; another judges adaptation by whether people thrive rather than by population growth alone. Dating apps can save time while changing commitment and social interaction. These are classroom interpretations, not universal accounts of Japan or the US.

## Apply the challenge to a venture

The April 7 brief is to **design a tech-enabled solution, as a global team, that helps societies adapt to demographic change**. Identify a specific affected person, validate the task and constraints, then choose technology. Japan is framed as a place to learn about problems that may later affect students' home markets, not a prediction that countries follow identical paths.
