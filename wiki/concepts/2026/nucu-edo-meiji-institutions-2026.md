# Edo and Meiji Institutional Reinvention in NUCU — 2026

The January 20, 2026 NUCU lecture uses **Edo (1603–1868)** and **Meiji (1868–1912)** to examine how institutions respond to outside technology. Its business analogy compares shogun, daimyo, samurai and peasants with leadership, regional management, a warrior class and labor. The model is a teaching interpretation with simplified history, not a current account of Japan or an independently checked chronology.

## Isolation as a tradeoff

The lecture traces Portuguese matchlock contact in **1543**, reverse engineering, Christian missionary activity from **1549**, religious repression and rebellion, and the roughly **1639–1853** sakoku period. It describes sovereignty and internal stability gained at the cost of exposure to industrial change abroad. **Dejima** trade with Dutch and Chinese partners qualifies its own “complete isolation” language.

**Perry's four black ships in 1853**, superior steam/cannon technology and unequal negotiations are the external shock. The **Convention of Kanagawa** represents forced opening in this account. Students debate whether the earlier policy failed or achieved a narrower goal of cultural continuity.

Isolation did not mean no invention. The lecture names **ukiyo-e**, the **Wave of Kanagawa**, **nigiri sushi** as fast food, **manga**, the **Myriad Clock**, and tea-serving/archer mechanical dolls. Connections between artistic mechanisms and present-day robot attitudes are speculative cultural interpretations.

## Reinvention involves institutions as well as tools

Meiji modernization combines central authority, compensation of domain leaders, a national army, national currency and currency taxation, outside learning and domestic capability. The lecture dates centralization to **1871** and highlights **1873** cash taxes, replacing fragmented rice-based obligations. It connects these revenues with investment and overseas study.

The **Iwakura Mission**, high-paid **oyatoi gaikokujin** experts, railways, telegraphs, schooling and public enterprises are mechanisms for acquiring and spreading knowledge. **“Japanese spirit, Western technology”** expresses selective adoption rather than a simple claim that Japan became Western. The lecturer's Prussia, company-history and territorial statements require verification; consult the detailed source's cautions before using them as historical evidence.

**Zaibatsu**, **Mitsubishi**, **Mitsui** and **Nintendo's 1889 playing-card origin** illustrate industrial organization and adaptation. The **1877 Satsuma Rebellion** represents resistance to displaced samurai roles. The source emphasizes that social contracts and identities can obstruct economic reorganization.

## Use the analogies critically

Discussion compares protectionism with Chinese-EV tariffs and Google protecting search revenues. It compares samurai displacement with AI automation, but students explicitly distinguish **government abolition of hereditary privilege** from technological change across many occupations. Compensation and retraining/transition pathways matter; the analogy does not settle whether displacement is justified.

The **Opium Wars** example frames the perceived cost of failing to modernize. Questions about identity, AI-written scripts and robot personhood are open inquiries. Observing shrines beside skyscrapers during the 2026 trip is an exercise in identifying continuity and change.
