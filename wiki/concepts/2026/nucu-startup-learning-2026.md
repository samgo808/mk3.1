# NUCU International Startup Learning — 2026

The archived 2026 NUCU program information describes experiential startup learning through international teamwork, AI and automation, and direct study of societal challenges in Japan. Students use lean canvas planning, design thinking, and primary market research to develop a business proposal and pitch it in Japan. This page preserves the source's 2026 teaching approach and anonymous participant perspectives; it is not a current 2027 syllabus.

## Learning methods and activities

International cohort teams, including Nagoya University students, develop startup ideas addressing shrinking populations. The source names lean startup tools and global market testing alongside lean canvas planning, design thinking, and primary market research. It specifically describes a **Design Thinking Sprint** to prototype solutions blending creativity and practicality.

Students are expected to move from concept toward execution through a complete business proposal. Workshops, lectures, expert-led sessions, field visits, field trips, and visits to companies and innovation hubs provide practical exposure. The final pitch at Nagoya University is described as involving business and healthcare entrepreneurship experts, academics, business leaders, and fellow student innovators. These descriptions do not establish a named panel or guarantee that a venture launched.

## Four challenge areas

- **Aging populations:** advanced elder care technology.
- **Labor shortages:** workforce automation.
- **Urban-rural disparities:** smart infrastructure adaptation.
- **Mental health:** digital tools supporting social stability.

Proposed technological approaches include AI, automation, advanced mobility, robotics, and other next-generation technologies. The source presents Japan's aging society, innovation, infrastructure, and digital transformation as a context for developing and testing ideas that improve health and quality of life. Applying lessons to eastern and western societies is part of the rationale; the source's numerical aging prediction is unsupported and should not be used as demographic evidence.

## Critical inquiry and career aims

Named discussion topics include **AI & Ethics**, **AI & Epistemology**, and **How to Know Truth**. The program advertises critical thinking, adaptability, purposeful innovation, leadership, practical innovation/automation skills, and exposure to global business and policy around AI. Cultural immersion and connections with peers, mentors, and international teammates are presented as personal and professional benefits, not independently verified outcomes.

## Participant perspectives and advice

Anonymous testimonials describe learning to collaborate across cultures, work without fixed guidelines, and approach open-ended problems flexibly. Participants mention site visits that broaden perspectives, curiosity stimulated by hands-on experience, market-survey skills, fieldwork, problem solving, business logistics, and connections useful in later life or work. One account describes discovering an interest in healthcare through entrepreneurship, strategy, and process improvement, together with practice in communication, design, and leadership. Others connect firsthand observation of shrinking/aging societies to future work in their home countries or towns, or describe discovering the global potential of entrepreneurship.

The four advice passages recommend:

1. Spend time understanding people's pain points and the problem before developing a specific, innovative idea.
2. Ask many questions about Japanese healthcare and advancement; be open to teamwork and develop emotional intelligence that supports collaboration.
3. Bring authenticity and expect both professional and personal growth.
4. Move beyond one's comfort zone and try different tools for an idea.

These are paraphrased participant reports, not measured learning results. The source gives no participant names or publication permissions. ## Program Guide: connecting field evidence to ideas

The **2026 Program Guide** adds concrete field-learning contexts: Google for Startups/Techstars Tokyo for mentorship and scaling; STATION Ai/Tongali for regional and university startup support; the hospital xR Center for simulation and clinical education; PREVENT for prevention and remote coaching; NCGG for evidence, safety and assistive robotics; and Professor Hirofumi Aoki for mobility and driving simulation.

Rural visits to Agi Clinic, Iwamura and Cyclamen connect primary/home care, population decline, transport access and cultural continuity. The guide asks teams to compare Tokyo, Nagoya, Obu and Nakatsugawa, gather stakeholder interviews and hospital surveys, organize user/patient/family needs, and synthesize findings into NIC presentations with faculty/stakeholder feedback. These are planned learning activities, not measured outcomes or authorization to publish interviewees' personal information.

The shared learning principle is to identify needs before choosing technology: biodesign in Professor Masakatsu Fukai's session, clinical problems in Dr. Shintaro Oyama's session, and user-centered evaluation at NCGG and the xR Center.
