# NUCU Japan Care Systems, Access and Dignity — 2026

The archived February 24, 2026 NUCU lecture frames aging as a medical, financial and social challenge. **Hasegawa-sensei** uses **Dr. Ryoichi Ichihashi's home-care approach** to distinguish living longer from living well. These are course concepts, not current care or insurance instructions.

## Two systems, different purposes

The lecture dates **universal medical coverage to 1961** and **Long-Term Care Insurance to 2000**, explicitly correcting an initial 2008 slip. It separately dates the medical-insurance program for people **75+** to **2008**. Medical care treats illness; long-term care also supports activities such as bathing, dressing, eating and moving safely.

Long-term care involves assessment, a care level, taxes and premiums, with **10–30% user cost** in the lecture. Its younger-age eligibility wording is unclear. The medical-copay figures contain overlapping age bands and a conflicting 3%/10% claim. See [[2026-02-24-nucu-session-7]] for the full source discrepancy; these figures should not determine a student's expected bill or coverage.

The price comparison asks **“30% of what?”** Japan's national fee schedule and income-related monthly caps are contrasted with US negotiated pricing, deductibles, coinsurance and network limits. Accessible visits still cost staff time and public money; affordable access and sustainability can pull in different directions.

## Home, family and community

The lecture gives three reasons for home care: the person's preference and familiar surroundings, potentially lower cost, and limited facility capacity. A family may still be unable to provide support or a person may need round-the-clock care. Public facilities are described as cheaper but with longer waits; private facilities may offer more places at higher prices.

Ichihashi's model combines doctors, nurses, dentists, dieticians and other professions. Its point is to understand a person's own wishes, not merely to prolong life. Care coordination and help for family caregivers can make a modest activity possible. Identifying patient medical details are redacted from the compiled source account.

## Research practical adoption barriers

Clinics may lack money, staff or time to adopt technology. A system designed for older people must fit their abilities and available infrastructure. The lecture proposes hospital-to-home coordination, caregiver support, staff efficiency and mobility as venture areas; it does not certify any prototype as safe or effective.

Urban buses, trains and taxis contrast with sparse rural transport. Giving up driving may improve road safety while making an older person isolated. Community buses, subsidized taxis, younger neighbors' help and autonomous transport trials are discussed as possible responses, with locations and rules unspecified.

The workforce problem involves both headcount and distribution: rural outmigration, unpopular geriatric specialties and urban clinic concentration. Universities sending doctors and targeted incentives are described as responses. Students should ask who bears the burden and how a proposed service preserves dignity, independence and human relationships. See [[nucu-shrinking-society-design-2026]].
